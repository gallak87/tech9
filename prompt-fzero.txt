# Goal

Build an original, high-octane, fast-paced, futuristic anti-gravity racing game inspired by the blistering speed, precision and high-stakes racing of F-Zero. Working title: VECTOR//NOVA. Target the browser. Three.js + Vite + ES modules is a strong default, but choose the engine, rendering stack and asset pipeline that best serve the result. Babylon.js, WebGPU where supported, Blender-authored assets, procedural modelling and other appropriate tools are welcome. Explain the choice, then build the game.

Build it as a standalone application in its own directory, such as games/vector-nova/, with its own package manifest, dependencies, runtime, scripts and asset pipeline. This repository contains two competing frameworks: the root tech9 framework and _tech9/. Do not adopt either framework, scaffold the game through either one, or depend on their runtime, launcher, module registry, sync tooling or build conventions. The existing projects below are references from which to borrow selected techniques or self-contained code, not a required architecture. The finished application must install, run and build independently from its own directory.

Treat this as a real AAA production with a focused, finishable scope. Deliver a complete, polished racing experience with coherent art direction, excellent handling and replay value. AAA is the quality bar for everything the player touches; it is not permission to promise an impossible quantity of unfinished content. Use original ships, tracks, names, branding, music and UI. Capture the genre's exhilaration without copying Nintendo assets or recreating its specific designs.

The visual target is premium contemporary science-fiction realism: beautifully designed hard-surface racing machines, physically based materials, convincing lighting, dense but readable environments, dramatic scale, deep perspective and strong atmospheric depth. No pixel art, painterly textures, flat low-poly stand-ins, toy proportions or generic neon primitives presented as finished art. Futurism must come from industrial design, architecture, technology and worldbuilding, not just emissive strips and bloom.

The first deliverable is a small, playable staging environment for my review of the art direction and racing feel. Show me one proposed ship silhouette in a representative 3D racing level as early as possible, then wait for my explicit approval before committing to that direction or expanding production. The full-game requirements below are the destination after that review, not prerequisites for showing me the first build.

The finished game must include:

- A complete racing loop: polished launch screen, ship selection and garage, track selection, race briefing, grid introduction, countdown, racing, finish, results, rewards, retry and championship progression. Include Quick Race, a three-race Championship and Time Trial with a local personal-best ghost. Save settings, records and unlocks locally, with graceful handling when storage is unavailable. No accounts or backend required.
- Three complete, distinctive circuits with different racing identities: a layered megacity expressway at blue hour; a storm-battered industrial energy complex; and a sunlit orbital shipyard above a planet. Each needs authored sightlines, memorable landmarks, trackside engineering, alternate racing lines and a dramatic signature section. Deliver full circuits with meaningful corners and elevation changes, not variations of a flat oval. Aim for roughly 60–120-second laps at race pace after playtesting.
- Real three-dimensional racing: banked sweepers, drops, climbs, tunnels, elevated intersections and selected wall rides, corkscrews or loops. Build these around a stable local track frame so the ship, collision, gravity and camera agree through every orientation. The route must feel physically constructed in a larger world, with supports, barriers, undersides, service structures and believable connections. Avoid visible scenery cutoffs and empty space immediately beyond the road.
- Four finished, selectable ships with distinct silhouettes, handling and sound: a nimble interceptor, a balanced all-rounder, a heavy bruiser and a volatile speed specialist. Give each a readable cockpit/canopy, layered hull panels, credible propulsion, material variation, visible hover hardware, animation, thruster effects and restrained wear. Differences in acceleration, maximum speed, grip, mass, energy and recovery must matter in play. Ship previews must show the actual game assets.
- A field of at least 12 racers including the player, with competent AI that follows the real track, brakes for corners, chooses lines, overtakes, avoids hazards, uses boost and makes recoverable mistakes. Include distinct rival tendencies and difficulty levels. Any catch-up assistance must be bounded and documented; opponents must not teleport or ignore the handling rules to manufacture a close race.
- High-octane, fast-paced racing with snappy, expressive handling: immediate steering response, stable high-speed control, independent airbraking or lateral lean, controlled slip, readable traction recovery, satisfying wall contact and a skill-based side attack. Build sustained intensity through close rival battles, rapid corner-to-corner decisions, daring overtakes and explosive but controllable boost exits, with enough visual anticipation to make mastery possible. Speed must come from movement through the world, track cadence, sound and camera behaviour together. Do not substitute excessive FOV, screen shake or particles for a convincing driving model. Make a single clean lap enjoyable before expanding content.
- A boost/energy risk-reward system: boosting consumes a shared energy reserve that also absorbs damage; recharge lanes reward deliberate line choice. Add readable boost strips, jumps, hazards and a small, coherent set of tactical pickups such as a brief shield, an energy cell and a charged impulse attack. Telegraph effects, enforce cooldowns and stacking rules, and preserve racing skill as the main determinant of success. Use a one-slot pickup system with a clear activation control. Keep Time Trial rules fixed, pickup-free and comparable between runs.
- Full racing feedback: engine load, hover resonance, airbrake effects, thruster expansion, speed-sensitive wind, sparks on contact, shield impacts, boost shockwaves, damage warnings, checkpoint timing, near-miss feedback and a strong finish-line payoff. Establish a visual hierarchy so the racing line and upcoming hazards remain legible at maximum speed. Every major event needs an appropriate sound and visual response.
- A finished presentation layer: restrained motorsport-inspired typography, crisp HUD, readable speed/energy/position/lap indicators, minimap, split times, clear pickup state and minimal racing-line assistance that can be disabled. Include smooth menus, proper loading and error states, pause/restart, complete keyboard menu navigation, gamepad support, remappable controls, dead-zone settings, volume controls, graphics presets and reduced-motion options. Support responsive mobile menus and usable touch racing controls with configurable size/handedness, safe-area spacing, orientation handling and clean input release on focus loss. Tune mobile rendering separately and report tested devices honestly. Explain controls before the first race. No decorative buttons that do nothing.

# How to work

1. Architecture first. Before feature code, make a short, targeted reference survey and write a concise initial ARCHITECTURE.md. Define one folder per subsystem: core/runtime, renderer, environment, track, ships, vehicle physics, input, camera, race simulation, AI, pickups/hazards, effects, audio, UI, progression/replays, content and verification tools. Define the shared data model, folder ownership, public APIs, event schemas, lifecycle and cleanup rules. Specify units (metres, seconds, +Y world up), coordinate conventions, local track frames, fixed-step simulation with render interpolation, collision strategy, checkpoint validation, seeded gameplay RNG and replay requirements. Define how race state, track data, rendering and presentation stay separate. Account for spline seams, gravity transitions, high-speed tunnelling, edge collisions, off-track recovery and camera clipping. Keep decorative failures isolated; surface essential failures clearly rather than silently running a broken race. Fully specify the contracts needed for the first staging build; sketch later systems and refine them after human review rather than spending the first milestone designing the entire production.

   Inspect these repository references before reinventing the relevant capabilities. Selective reuse is encouraged wherever it improves quality or saves work; none of these references dictates the engine, visual style, game design or world layout:

   - games/vulpine/: study procedural ship generation, especially src/render/geobuild.js and src/ships/ with ship-materials.js. Borrow techniques for lofted hulls, shaped surfaces, panel detail, canopies, material separation and silhouette readability. Extend or replace those techniques with Blender modelling, baking and export where useful. Design an original anti-gravity racing craft; do not transplant Vulpine's recognisable aircraft silhouette, flight/combat systems or game runtime.
   - games/chronoforge-echo/: study menu behaviour, keyboard navigation, focus management, input routing, touch controls, mobile layout and lifecycle handling. Useful entry points include src/ui.js, src/ui-session.js, src/touch-input.js, src/touch-controls.js, src/mobile-lifecycle.js, src/mobile-preferences.js, src/viewport.js and the mobile/touch CSS. Adapt the interaction patterns to racing and the new visual language. Its RPG rules, 2D rendering, artwork and screen layout are not templates for this game.
   - _tech9/baselines/web-3d-world/: inspect the running-scene implementation and its rendering, lighting, atmosphere, settings, asset loading, resource ownership and verification patterns. This is a reference demo, not the application's scaffold. Extract useful ideas or bounded helpers into the new application without bringing over SceneApp, the layer registry or the framework's launch/build/sync machinery.
   - _tech9/modules/weather/ and _tech9/modules/landscape/: consider reusable weather/material response, atmosphere, terrain rendering, instancing, detail management, shadows and Blender asset preparation. These are specialised forest/terrain implementations. Their trees, ground layout, weather presets, scale and artistic assumptions are optional; the racer should remain free to use megastructures, industrial tracks, orbital environments or another approved direction. In particular, their heightfield and downward surface queries do not solve stacked track surfaces, wall rides, loops or arbitrary track-local gravity. Borrow appropriate rendering techniques and replace incompatible assumptions with racing-specific logic.

   Record the few parts reused, adapted or deliberately replaced, along with source paths, licences, dependencies and compatibility limitations. Bring any adopted code/assets into the application's ownership and preserve required notices. Resolve dependencies locally; do not add runtime imports, symlinks or build dependencies back into either framework or the reference games. Do not modify the reference projects to make this game work. A brief reason is enough when a reference does not fit; there is no quota for reusing code and no need to audit the entire repository before the staging build.

   Include an art bible and asset policy: silhouettes, shape language, material palette, lighting references, architectural scale, UI style and VFX hierarchy. Use assets you create or assets with verified redistribution rights; prefer original and CC0 assets. Record provenance and licences in an asset manifest. Blender may produce models, baked detail, collision proxies, LODs and textures for browser-ready export. AI-generated imagery may support concepts or suitable textures if available, but rendered gameplay must be a genuine interactive 3D scene. Never bake an attractive screenshot into the background and call it a game.

   Establish measurable browser budgets: target sustained 60 fps at 1920×1080 on an explicitly identified representative desktop configuration. Use 16.7 ms as the steady-state frame budget and report frame-time percentiles and long frames, not just average fps. Set initial budgets around 1,000 visible draw calls, 2 million visible triangles and 512 MB of estimated resident GPU assets on the high preset; profile and justify adjustments. Target a usable initial experience within approximately 30 MB compressed, with additional circuits streamed separately. Reuse assets, instance repeating geometry, use LODs, compress textures and meshes, and warm shaders before racing. Choose a broadly usable browser baseline and clearly document unsupported configurations. If using WebGPU-specific features, provide a supported fallback or an explicit capability gate. Never assume hardware capabilities that were not tested.

2. Build the verification loop before the game. Create a browser automation harness that starts the app, waits for an explicit ready signal, loads a seeded scenario, drives real controls, selects a camera preset and writes PNGs plus JSON evidence. Capture console errors, failed asset loads, active settings, race state, lap/checkpoint data, frame-time percentiles, draw calls and visible geometry. Add deterministic input playback and short gameplay recordings or frame sequences so reviewers can inspect motion, steering, corner exits, boost transitions, collisions and recovery. Static screenshots cannot prove handling quality. Report the actual test environment; headless or software-rendered timings are not evidence of desktop GPU performance.

   Every module must expose a representative showcase and a reproducible test scenario. Include close ship inspection, a fast corner, a bank/gravity transition, the busiest race start, an impact, a pickup interaction and each circuit's signature vista. Exercise the full menu-to-race-to-results-to-retry flow. Check resize, focus loss, pause/resume, missing gamepad and unavailable storage. No agent may claim a visual result it has not captured and inspected, or claim racing feel it has not exercised in motion. If an essential tool or environment is unavailable, mark the relevant check unverified.

   Keep preparation bounded. Implement only the capture/readiness/logging controls needed for the staging build first; add the remaining scenarios alongside the corresponding features. The first playable handling benchmark is also the human review build in step 3. Temporary blockouts are development tools; they cannot pass a final art gate or remain in the finished game.

3. Mandatory early human review. Make this the first playable milestone, immediately after the lean architecture and basic capture harness. Build a local staging environment containing one original, drivable ship silhouette and a compact, approximately 30–45-second repeatable 3D racing circuit. Include a fast straight, a meaningful corner, a banked section, an elevation change and a short tunnel or overpass so I can judge speed, spatial depth, route readability and camera behaviour. Provide responsive steering, braking/airbraking, boost, basic collision/recovery, restart and a minimal control legend. The review build must be playable through real controls, not just an automatic flythrough.

   Give the ship a deliberately designed silhouette with enough canopy, propulsion and material definition to communicate its identity at racing distance. Include a neutral inspection/turntable view and the actual chase-camera view. Give one compact section of track representative near-final materials, lighting, barriers/supports and a strong distant landmark, with readable foreground, middle distance and background. The remaining staging geometry may be clearly labelled provisional. Include basic engine/boost/wind feedback so the handling can be judged in context. Keep the world small enough to revise cheaply but visually representative enough to approve or reject the proposed aesthetic; a blank test plane with a box ship is insufficient.

   Bring me the playable URL, controls, a ship inspection image, an in-race image and a short motion capture if the tools support it. State which elements are representative, which are provisional and the specific direction you propose for ship design, environment, camera and racing feel. Ask for approval or requested changes to those choices. An optional quick critic pass may flag obvious problems, but do not run a prolonged gauntlet or wait for a 9/10 score before showing this first build. The human review is an early direction decision, not a final release gate.

   Stop at this review and wait for my explicit response. Do not start additional ships/circuits, the full championship, production menu polish or broad asset generation while approval is pending. Silence, elapsed time and an automated critic's score are not approval. If I request changes, revise the same staging build and present it again before expanding scope. Record the review state, feedback and approved direction in docs/STATUS.json and update the art bible after approval. This requested human checkpoint is an explicit exception to autonomous execution and the rule against routine questions. Approval authorises continued production; it does not waive the final quality bar.

4. Fan out. Use available multi-agent orchestration. One builder agent per module, owning only its folder. One integrator agent owns core, shared schemas, dependency/configuration changes and cross-module wiring. Builders submit explicit core-change requests to the integrator. Before human approval, delegate only narrowly scoped work needed for the staging build; broad production fan-out begins after approval. Run in dependency-ordered waves: (1) runtime contracts, verification harness, rendering baseline, track frames, input, vehicle physics and camera, developed just far enough for the staging review; (2) after approval, the finished ship pipeline, race simulation, AI, pickups/hazards, environment, effects and audio; (3) the three circuits, garage, menus, HUD, progression and replays; (4) complete-game integration, optimisation and polish. Respect available concurrency rather than spawning more agents than the environment supports. Use contracts and representative fixtures so work can proceed independently without competing edits.

   Gate content expansion on explicit human approval of the playable staging build. Carry that approved visual and handling direction into each builder's brief. Integrate continuously; do not wait until every folder is finished to discover that movement, art, camera and effects disagree. If multi-agent tools are unavailable, explicitly report that limitation and perform the roles sequentially without pretending independent agents or judges ran.

5. Gauntlet every module. After human direction approval, apply the formal production gauntlets. After each builder round, a separate critic agent acts as a brutal AAA art director, racing designer and technical reviewer. The critic writes no implementation code and does not grade from the builder's description. It loads the app itself, takes its own screenshots, inspects close, medium and distant views, and tests relevant gameplay in motion. Use legal, clearly attributed references from premium futuristic racing and contemporary AAA vehicle/environment rendering. Compare actual gameplay views at comparable scale and settings; concept art or prerendered cinematics are not equivalent evidence.

   Critique concrete defects: cheap silhouettes, weak materials, floating geometry, flat lighting, absent contact shadows, inadequate depth cues, excessive bloom, repetitive scenery, empty track edges, poor racing-line readability, floaty controls, input lag, camera snapping, uncontrolled slip, clipping, unfair AI, untelegraphed damage, weak sound, unfinished menus and frame-time spikes. Demand fixes that improve the actual experience, not extra features that conceal unfinished fundamentals.

   Score relevant categories separately from 0–10: art direction and asset quality; lighting/materials/spatial depth; handling and camera; track design and readability; race systems and AI; audio/VFX feedback; UI/onboarding/accessibility; and performance/stability. Mark genuinely inapplicable module categories N/A with a reason; all categories apply to the whole game. Anchors: 10 = exceptional shipped AAA quality, 9 = convincing premium release quality with only minor remaining issues, 8 = polished but visibly below the target, 7 = good indie, 5 = prototype/programmer art. A pass requires every applicable category to score at least 9/10, zero uncaught runtime errors or missing required assets, no critical gameplay defects, and a verified pass on the agreed performance target. A high average cannot conceal weak handling or unfinished art.

   Every review returns evidence paths, the category scores, pass/fail and a ranked issue list. Each issue needs a reproduction or screenshot/timecode, player impact, responsible module and measurable acceptance condition. The builder fixes the highest-impact issues first; the critic reruns the affected scenario and checks for regressions. Each module's gauntlet stops at its first verified pass or after 10 completed critique rounds, whichever comes first. Count the initial critique as round 1. Never reset a counter, rename a module or quietly lower the bar to obtain more attempts.

6. Final gate. Once modules are integrated, a whole-game critic runs the complete experience with fresh eyes: first launch and onboarding, all ships, all three circuits, a complete championship, Time Trial and ghost playback, collisions, depletion/recovery, finish/results, restart, settings persistence and quality presets. Verify keyboard, gamepad, responsive layouts and mobile touch controls on actual available devices; distinguish browser emulation from physical-device testing and flag unavailable hardware honestly. Review the densest racing scenario as well as the beauty shots. Check that ships, lighting, track scale, audio, UI and VFX belong to the same world.

   Run a separate whole-game gauntlet with the same rubric: every category at least 9/10 and no blocking functional or performance failures, or a maximum of 10 whole-game critique rounds, whichever comes first. This is a finite release gate, not an endless polish loop. Then, when independent reviewers are available, give blind judges shuffled A/B pairs of our gameplay and comparable real reference gameplay, without identifying which is ours. Include motion clips for feel and screenshots for visual quality. Ask which looks more credible and why; record disagreement and reference sources. These comparisons inform the verdict but cannot replace functional testing or justify a fabricated score. If judges are unavailable, record the blind comparison as not performed.

   Deliver the complete runnable project, source assets and export scripts where applicable, a README with launch/build commands and controls, the architecture/art bible, asset licences, performance captures and the final critique report. Run a production build and smoke-test the built game, not only the development server. Leave the game easy to launch and playable from its real title screen.

7. /loop until every critic passes or the applicable 10-round cap is reached. This production loop starts only after the mandatory human direction approval; it must never bypass or automatically satisfy that checkpoint. Treat /loop as a workflow instruction, not a dependency on a particular slash command. Persist human review state, approved direction, evidence, category scores, iteration counters, current asset/performance budgets, verified test environments and ranked open issues to docs/STATUS.json after each round. Resume from the weakest failing area instead of restarting or rebuilding working systems. After a cap is reached, stop that gauntlet and preserve the remaining failures in the final report. Finish packaging and verification within the existing scope; do not claim the target was achieved when it was not. Report the actual result, whether that is a verified 9/10 release candidate or a playable build with unresolved shortcomings.

# Rules

- Use neither the root tech9 framework nor the _tech9 framework. Borrow useful reference techniques selectively, keep adopted code/assets self-contained, preserve provenance and keep the new application's runtime and build independent.
- Make the playable staging environment the first deliverable and obtain my explicit approval of its art direction and feel before production expansion. Keep early work small and reversible. Do not let architecture work, asset generation or automated critique postpone this human checkpoint.
- Never inflate scores, fabricate reviewers, invent performance numbers or claim checks that were not run. Report real scores, failed rounds, missing features and limitations. The final 9/10 threshold is an evidence requirement, not an instruction to award 9/10.
- Never edit another builder's folder. Route core changes through the integrator. Define ownership of shared assets and contracts before parallel work begins.
- Keep the dev server running and the app loadable while agents are inspecting it. Integrate in small, recoverable steps and fix broken boot or broken racing before adding content.
- Keep the implementation genuinely three-dimensional and interactive. No prerecorded gameplay, screenshot backgrounds masquerading as environments, fake speed readouts, scripted victories or scenery-only demos.
- Prioritise handling, camera, track readability and stable frame pacing throughout production. Graphics and effects must reinforce the race. Motion blur, chromatic aberration, shake, distortion and bloom need restrained defaults and appropriate controls.
- Use physically coherent material response, correct colour management and consistent world scale. Preserve highlight detail and material identity. Do not use post-processing to hide low-quality geometry, textures or lighting.
- Ship complete, coherent features. Do not trade three polished circuits and four finished ships for a larger collection of unfinished content. No placeholder art, silent major actions, empty menus or nonfunctional settings in a passing build.
- Make race outcomes and records trustworthy: ordered checkpoints, valid lap timing, no finish-line shortcuts, correct reset penalties and ghost compatibility/versioning. Pause and tab suspension must not corrupt the simulation or produce giant catch-up steps.
- Honour browser constraints with measured optimisation, staged loading and clear support requirements. Do not claim universal device support. Provide useful feedback when loading, recovering from an error or encountering unsupported hardware.
- Use available tools autonomously, including Blender or asset-generation tools when they improve the result. If a tool is missing, choose a viable alternative and state the impact. Do not pretend unavailable tools were used.
- Apart from the mandatory early human review, do not ask me routine design or implementation questions. Make sensible decisions, record assumptions and keep going within the approved direction. Request additional input only for a genuinely blocking permission, credential or external dependency that cannot be resolved within the authorised scope.
